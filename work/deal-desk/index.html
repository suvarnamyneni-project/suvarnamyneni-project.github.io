Who I worked with
Product, engineering on the transaction and accounting services, operations, accounting, transaction and closing analysts, compliance, and production support.

The problem

A paid deal lived in several systems, and in the notes between them.

The program set out to deliver an AI-native transaction platform for the US and Canada, designed to replace TRX and SkySlope. In the work I watched, a Transaction Analyst still settled a deal by moving between Task Center, Deal Desk, document AI, and SkySlope. Fee details sat in free-text notes. Staff re-keyed them. After a file said settled, an accounting error could stay invisible to the person the agent calls.

Agents already felt that fragmentation. To move a file forward they filled fields that did not apply to the deal, sometimes with placeholder text, because the screen would not accept the document until the form was complete. MLS data did not always arrive with the file, so they typed it again. A rejected document did not ping them. Staff could not write to the agent inside the tool, so missing items went out as a hand-built email. Canada’s closing analysts were still sharing spreadsheets with finance.

How might a Transaction Analyst take one deal from submission to the accounting hand-off, with every number traceable and every error visible?

  • Checking, then deciding Most of the effort was comparing dates and amounts across a document and the deal. The judgment was the short part.
  • Handoffs that waited Work passed from a coordinator to an analyst and got checked twice. The wait did not add a decision.
  • Where is my deal? Status was hard to see, so agents asked support. The old payment question ran from the agent, to support, to the analyst, to accounting.
  • Exceptions were the job Mismatches, a failed posting, and a changed close date were ordinary. Recovery was ad hoc.
  • Regions are different products Canada uses a lawyer or conveyancer, firm and closed stages, and a closing analyst who settles after the analyst prepares. A label swap would have lied.
  • Alerts had lost their meaning In the August 6, 2026 settlement demo, the group named 80 critical alerts blocking automatic disbursement authorizations.

Status truth was the loudest theme in the staff idea marketplace I reviewed: 15 of 28 active tensions sat on this desk, and the hottest was a file approved in document AI that could still show the wrong status in SkySlope and Task Center. In that same August demo, an August launch in Arkansas was postponed because bugs and feature requests were still open, and stakeholders worried the new process would add screens. Leadership also priced the SkySlope document library and APIs at about $12,000 a year, about 1% of that vendor bill, and chose a phased interface: a new UI, with that library and those APIs kept as the backend for a time.

    One sheet is 14 numbered settlement journeys. Another is a to-be swimlane for every transaction type, from listing to cancel. I used both. They are related, and they are not the same document.

  4. 04

Who touches a deal

19 personas, agents and staff.

Five agent personas and fourteen staff personas, from listing and buyer’s agents through compliance, the analyst, Canada’s closing analyst, production support, and post-close. I used the set as shared language with product and operations. A transaction coordinator is agent-hired, a payee on the file, and a different person from the Transaction Analyst. Systems count as actors too: document AI, SkySlope, Task Center, the accounting service, Oracle, and Enterprise, where mentor configuration lives. Of the 57 settlement steps, 20 are fully automated, and most failures sit in the hand-off between those actors.

How a deal moves

14 journeys, then a wider lifecycle.

I led the journey map in Miro and flagged an open question on any step where the rule, the owner, or the source of truth was still unsettled. Those flags mattered as much as the happy path. A later check of the prototype against that sheet came out 7 covered, 6 partial, and 1 missing.

The to-be workflow map is wider than those 14 journeys. It swims a listing, a sale, a lease, four kinds of referral, a broker price opinion, ShowGo, and a catch-all for fees and installments, across the agent, the system, the analyst, a transactions manager, broker compliance, and the managing broker. Most of the lighter types are meant to auto-settle. The analyst handles the exception. Settlement itself has two phases: first the analyst settles from a Ready for Settlement queue, then an approved file can auto-settle, with an Unable to Settle queue when it cannot. A post-close correction is a single-file delta, routed to a correction analyst. A withdrawn sale can come back as a reactivated listing.

Across the settlement journeys I counted 57 steps. In the demo, 38 were live, 10 were in progress, 5 were proposed, and 4 were still an open decision.

What I took from it

Six things the desk had to respect.

  1. 01

    The work is not in one place

    Analysts still left the desk for a check copy and for notes. A faster screen that sends them somewhere else is a slower process.

  2. 02

    Notes were the hidden integration

    Advances, extended hand, and coordinator fees lived in SkySlope notes. The change log already recorded the review. The notes were a second, worse copy.

  3. 03

    Settled hid the failure

    A variance or an accounting failure could happen after Settle while the analyst still saw a finished file. The unpaid-agent call starts there.

  4. 04

    Money is provisional until it settles

    Commission and cap depend on close order. Anything shown before settlement has to say it is an estimate, including why the number might move.

  5. 05

    One shell, two countries

    In Canada the analyst prepares and the closing analyst settles, and overrides want an approval. The US demo revised approvals so they fire on triggers, such as a company-commission change or a personal sale over the threshold.

  6. 06

    Parallel lanes, one “who has the ball”

    The October workshop described agent, broker compliance, analyst, and trust accounting moving at once. A clear-to-settle set, including a W-9 when a referral is paid, can block the disbursement authorization even when another lane is done. The file can still settle with an open compliance item, then sit in a separate review bucket until a broker approval archives it.

With product and operations I held five principles: one desk, validate before you act, guide the fix, status always visible, and one design system for agent and staff. The rule on every AI and validation gate is the same. The system checks. A person decides.

The Transaction Analyst desk

From the queue to disbursed, on one file.

I designed the spine from submit through disbursement, with the exception lane drawn at the same time as the happy path. Validation gates and document AI sit on the file. Legacy accounting and the document system stayed visible, because pretending they were gone would have made the status a lie.

Status names disagreed across the sources: Settle, Mark Disbursed, Disbursed, Settlement Complete, Closed (Paid). I built one lifecycle and an alias table so product, engineering, and operations could point at the same moment. On the staff side the states run from created, to ready for the disbursement authorization, sent, waiting on closing documents, documents received, settlement started, settled, and closed. Withdrawn and in correction stay on that same file.

Queue

Oldest work first, and the next action on the row.

The queue is role-based: analyst, compliance, and operations each see their own work. Filters follow the lifecycle, including new from the agent, needs documents, needs action, ready for the disbursement authorization, and in settlement. A row carries status, stage, and the next action, so the analyst can see an aging file without opening it. The journey sheet still wanted an explicit oldest-first sort. I kept that as a gap, not as a feature I would claim was done.

Opening a file shows one header: the parties, the close date, the price, and the blockers that still lock Send. Tabs carry counts. Missing information sits in context, such as a coordinator who has not been named. The October schema session added a rule I folded in here: a staff task is an approval step, and it should appear when approval is what remains. Non-approval steps can be marked complete, not needed, or skipped with a comment. Approval is the step that cannot be skipped. A listing stays as the core workflow. A sale is an attempt that can be discarded and replaced if the offer falls through.

Checklist before money

Intake is documents first. The analyst can ask the agent for what is missing. That request shows up on the agent’s home as something that needs them. Marking the checklist complete moves the file toward compliance. Financials stay locked until that gate, or until the demo path says compliance can review in parallel.

Compliance, then the analyst

Compliance sees needs review, escalated, and approved. They can approve, send the file back to the agent or the analyst with a reason, or escalate. Settlement stays view-only for them. A send-back returns the file to analyst intake.

Alerts and mismatches

Two sources, one decision. The analyst picks.

Critical alerts explain the problem and point at the fix. A mismatch between the agent’s value and the extracted value opens a compare: who entered each number, when, and where in the document the extract came from. The analyst picks one. Commission recalculates from that choice. If they are not sure, they ask the agent. The choice stays visible and reversible, with a timestamp, because the audit trail is the point.

Which source wins by default is still open. I left that undecided in the interface instead of inventing a winner. Informational alerts never block. The mentor example is the clearest: under a $250 minimum the fee is waived, the math is shown, and the alert says it does not block Send.

Financials, disbursement, settlement

The gates fail closed, and they say how to open.

Financials are prefilled from the agent desk and from extracts. The analyst edits what changed. Bonuses and concessions come from the contract. The advance, extended hand, and coordinator fee are structured lines, with a source on each one, because those used to live in notes. The August demo’s rules are on the file: a $100 fee on a non-preferred third-party advance, a $50 extended-hand fee, and the mentor percent pulled from the agent profile so nobody opens another system to see it. Multiple parties and team splits are supported. Ownership-percentage splits waited until operations confirmed the rule.

Send stays disabled until compliance has approved and the blockers are clear. Every Send control says why: waiting on compliance, N blockers still open, or the authorization was already sent. The package confirms payees, roles, amounts, and method, and it checks that payees total the expected funds. Expected funds come from financials. There is no second entry. A changed close date creates a revised version. A skip covers a file that does not need an authorization. Version history stays on the file.

The package

Draft template, closing entity, expected funds, and a payee total that has to match.

Confirm, then wait for funds

Confirming the package moves the file to sent. Settlement waits on the money. The message thread stays available.

Settlement adds the payment. A check read by document AI is tagged as extracted. A row the analyst types is tagged as entered by them. Dates have to match before settle unlocks. The balance has to match. In the demo, a $500 underpayment is the case that should keep the file from being called reconciled. I recommended that disbursed be allowed only at a zero variance, or at an approved write-off. An unsigned or unmatched document can be re-uploaded or overridden, and the override is visible.

Awaiting funds

Mark disbursed

Once disbursed, the file is read-only. Corrections go out as a post-close correction with a reason, a new version, and the history intact, because accounting has already been told. The analyst can report an issue, which flags a post-settlement error for production support and leaves the file locked. The task then leaves the active queue. Today that error flag is still a manual report. The proposal, from the demo, is an informational, time-boxed flag that support clears when they mark the error manually handled, visible to the support team so an agent is not the first monitor.

The agent side of the same desk

The same timeline, and a home that leads with attention.

Agents were unhappy with SkySlope’s upload flow and with questions that did not apply. The agent desk lives inside the agent platform, on the same visual system as staff. Home leads with what needs them, then an estimated commission that says why it might change, and a timestamp for how fresh the numbers are. Inbox is one conversation per transaction, professional parties only. Submitting to the desk is one step. The deal page shows the same status timeline staff use, which is the answer to “where is my deal?”

Creating a deal can start from a document or from scratch. Document AI reads what it can, the agent reviews it, and then they submit. A missing-document notification has to land on the filtered checklist. That was a confirmed bug in the old path, and it is a requirement on this one. Wizard order stays fluid. Money-first and property-first are both real.

System thinking

The screen had to tell the truth about the systems behind it.

Engineering owns the accounting service. I designed the controls a Transaction Analyst sees, including when a control has to wait, explain itself, or stay hidden. These are the rules I designed against, in plain language.

  1. 01

    One transaction record

    The program’s rule is a single source of truth for price, commission, and dates. Accounting, commission, and Task Center read that record. They do not read the raw document or the legacy system as a second authority. Provenance chips exist because document AI, the agent, and staff all write, and trust is seeing who said what.

  2. 02

    Where the rules live

    A decision document weighed two homes for settlement rules. Keep them in TRX for the first release, which is faster and keeps the current invoice path. Or build an accounting service with template-driven rules. The document argued that path could cut duplicated rules by more than 90%, and that it was too heavy for the first release. The recommendation was to stay on TRX for the MVP and design the accounting service in parallel. By the August demo, that service was creating receivables and payables and comparing line totals to the aggregate before anything went to Oracle. The interface had to stay honest about which system was in charge that week.

  3. 03

    Variance stops the hand-off

    Variance is the gap between the actual total on a payable or receivable and the expected amount at settlement. If any record is off, the whole submission downstream is blocked, so a bad number cannot travel quietly. A tiny rounding remainder is treated as zero so a clean file is not blocked by how computers store decimals. A real shortfall is a failure, stored, and shown to production support.

  4. 04

    Retry and restart are different

    If a middle step fails, and later steps depend on it, the run stops. Recovery is a restart from that point. If a last step fails, and nothing after it depends on it, retry touches only the failed records. A success response that omits data a later step needs counts as a failure. The desk’s job is to make those two actions look different, and to show a reference to the payload without showing the payload itself.

  5. 05

    Migration had to be reversible

    An agent is flagged in Enterprise as using the new calculator instead of TRX. TRX ignores that agent’s incoming SkySlope transactions. The first release starts with a one-agent smoke test: their cap-period deals copy over, stay view-only in TRX, and raise the settlement task in the new tool. If settlement fails, the flag comes off, TRX goes back to edit, and the new tasks are deleted. Later waves use a file of beta agents, then residential states, commercial, and the referral division. A deal whose co-agent is not in the program stays out. Thousands of in-flight files, and about 19,800 pre-contracts, are part of the product, not a cleanup project for later.

  6. 06

    Settled is append-only

    A disbursed file does not get edited in place. A correction is a new post-close record with a reason. The change log records who did what. Migration itself needs the same habit: who ran a conversion, when, what was skipped, and a business sign-off. Task Center stays a separate app, so a task deep-links back with the role and the transaction still attached.

A few constraints shaped the UI more than any visual preference. Association forms cannot be blank templates we draft ourselves, because of how those forms are licensed, so a forms partner does the legal document and our AI checks the data. Document AI is asynchronous, on the order of 30 seconds, so the screen needs a real processing state. Canada’s accounting integration had a March 2027 constraint, so Canadian steps can be visible before that backend is finished. Document AI being down is a named risk: until it can read a check, the analyst still types the check from the checklist, and the field shows that a person entered it.

How I used AI

Three places AI showed up: in the research, in the prototypes, and in the product.

The tools and the product feature are different jobs. I used AI to read a large archive and to make clickable desks. The product uses AI to read a document and stop before the decision. I kept those jobs separate so a fast prototype could not quietly become a business rule.

  1. 01

    Grok, for the reading

    I used Grok to work through the program archive, the war-room notes, and the journey material, and to pull rules, conflicts, and open questions into a form I could design against. Every rule went back to the source before it reached a screen. Miro stayed the place the journeys and the ecosystem map lived: SkySlope into TRX, then the payment queue, then payables, plus the deep link from a SkySlope file into splits with the person’s role still attached.

  2. 02

    Claude, Lovable, Cursor, GitHub

    Claude Design held the visual system and the long canvases. Agent, analyst, and compliance were each explored as a 105-page file, plus a seven-slide happy path from an agent submit to a disbursed file on the broker view. One deck filled in its own placeholder splits and fees. I treated those figures as stand-ins.

  3. 03

    A clickable desk, the same day

    On October 5, 2026 I built the combined Agent and Staff prototype in Lovable: one shared state, a role switcher, and a path from a new purchase through intake, compliance, disbursement, and the broker pipeline. The same day I removed the coordinator as a switchable staff role, and I put a reason under every disabled Send control.

I then wrote a Cursor brief to rebuild that mock as a local app a teammate could clone from GitHub and run: Agent and Transaction Analyst, queues, disbursement issues, settlement alerts, and retry and restart on the downstream accounting pipeline. Mock data only. No backend, and no secrets. The brief’s test was simple. A fresh clone installs, runs, and builds.

Inside the product, AI checks and a person decides

Document AI classifies a file, pulls dates and amounts, and checks them against the deal. Compliance checks can flag a missing signature or initial and hold the file until the agent uploads a correction. The analyst sees both values and picks. Upload by the agent, full extraction on every file, and splitting a combined statement-and-check file are planned, not done. The long-term workflow map goes further: an AI reviewer comments and flags first, and auto-approves later. Auto-settle is phase two, when the clear-to-settle documents match and nothing is flagged. Phase one is still a person, with an SLA and an assignee on the task. Review starts on the first document, not on a finished package, because finance wants a new file visible for forecasting immediately.

  • Read The document is classified and the dates, amounts, and check details are extracted.
  • Check Extracted values are compared with the deal. Missing signatures and missing conditional documents are flagged.
  • Choose A person picks the value, asks the agent, or overrides with the override recorded. Settle stays locked until the checks that matter have passed.

Outcomes and what is next

What the desk is aimed at. The score comes after launch.

This work was still in prototype and demo in the sources I have. I am not going to invent a before-and-after on minutes per deal, screens per settlement, or support contacts. What I can account for is the shape of the design, and the counts from the maps and the audits.

  • 19 personas Five agent roles and fourteen staff roles, plus the systems I treated as actors.
  • 14 journeys 7 covered, 6 partial, 1 missing in the prototype check.
  • 57 settlement steps 38 live in the demo, 10 in progress, 5 proposed, 4 open.
  • 40 flows Each branch, from a mismatch to a post-settlement error, has a decision and a next step.
  • 29 discovery docs Read with the war rooms, the demo, and the program archive.
  • 15 of 28 tensions Staff idea-marketplace items that sit on this desk. Status truth was the hottest.

On the journey sheet, the demo flow reaches the accounting service in about 3 seconds. That is a demo-path timing, not a production service level. I also logged 24 open questions and 10 places the sources disagreed, so the conflict stayed visible.

The design is aimed at fewer screens per deal, one desk for agents and staff, less manual checking, a status anyone on the file can see, and a faster path through a guided fix, a retry, or a clear escalation. Those are intentions until we measure them. The measures I want after launch are screens per deal, time to settle, and how often an agent has to ask where the deal is.

What I would do next

  1. 01

    The real Settle step

    A confirm, a payment date, a demonstrated block when the close dates disagree, and one name for the end state.

  2. 02

    Errors after “settled”

    An automatic, time-boxed flag in Deal Desk and Task Center, visible to support, cleared when someone marks it manually handled.

  3. 03

    Approval, and Canada

    Manager approval on the US triggers, an approve-all path for Canada, and the closing-analyst flow taken from the spreadsheet walkthroughs.

  4. 04

    The file that is two documents

    Statement and check in one upload, once the rule is decided, plus the workflow guide on the file and a change log that shows the old value, the new value, and the reason.

Still open, and I would rather leave them open than guess: which source wins when document AI and the deal disagree; whether a combined file is one task or two; how long a post-settlement flag stays up and who clears it; when document AI will read the check, and what stays in SkySlope until then; whether a manual note still has a job beside the log; and how an overpayment works. The sources describe underpayment and a non-zero variance. They do not describe the overpayment path.

Reflection

What I will carry to the next desk.

  1. 01

    Map the systems, not only the screens

    The pain sat in the hand-offs. A webhook, a control, a send to accounting. Those systems had to be actors in the journey, or the screen would hide the failure.

  2. 02

    Write the rule before the picture

    Estimate until settled. Append-only after. The coordinator is not the analyst. Those three sentences prevented a class of wrong screens.

  3. 03

    Design the exception first

    The happy path is short. Trust is the retry, the escalate, the not-paid flag, and the correction that does not rewrite history.

  4. 04

    A fast prototype can be confidently wrong

    Lovable and Claude made a desk I could click the same day. The journey sheet was the thing that kept those clicks honest. Placeholder fees from a model are not a fee schedule.

  5. 05

    Leave the disagreement on the table

    Open questions, with an owner, moved faster than a polished screen that had silently picked a side. Deferring the unsettled split was a decision. So was extending a teammate’s prototype instead of starting over.
